More beginner projects go wrong from measuring and marking errors than from cutting itself — a precise cut on an inaccurate mark is still an inaccurate cut.

## Core Habits

1. **Measure twice, cut once.** It's a cliché because it works — a second check catches transposed numbers or misread marks before they become permanent.
2. **Use a sharp marking tool.** A thick pencil line can introduce enough error to matter on tight joints; a fine pencil or marking knife gives a more precise line.
3. **Mark your cut line, not just a point.** Use a square to draw a full line across the board rather than a single tick mark, which is easier to follow accurately with a saw.
4. **Account for blade thickness (kerf).** When cutting multiple pieces to length, decide whether your mark represents the cut line itself or the edge of the waste side, and stay consistent.
5. **Work from a single reference edge.** Measuring repeatedly from the same "true" edge, rather than from the last cut, prevents accumulated error across multiple pieces.

## Common Mistakes

- Mixing up which side of the line is "waste," leading to pieces that are slightly too short.
- Using a warped or bent measuring tape, introducing small but compounding errors.
- Not squaring your reference edge first, causing measurement lines to be off-angle from the start.

## A Simple Accuracy Checklist

1. Confirm your reference edge is actually straight and square.
2. Mark with a square, not just a pencil dot.
3. Double-check the measurement before cutting.
4. Cut just outside your line, then sand or trim to the exact mark if precision matters.

## Why This Skill Compounds

Accurate measuring and marking is the single skill that most affects whether joints fit cleanly and furniture ends up square. A structured woodworking plans library provides exact, pre-calculated measurements for each cut, which is a great way to build confidence while this skill develops.
