You don't need advanced joinery to build strong, good-looking projects — a handful of basic joints cover the vast majority of beginner and intermediate builds.

## Core Joints to Learn First

1. **Butt joint.** Two pieces joined at a simple right angle, usually reinforced with glue and screws or nails. Fast and forgiving, but the weakest joint on its own.
2. **Rabbet joint.** A notch cut into one piece so the other fits into it, increasing glue surface area and hiding some end grain — common in box and cabinet construction.
3. **Dado joint.** A groove cut into a piece to hold a shelf or panel, common in bookshelves and cabinets for strong, self-aligning assembly.
4. **Pocket-hole joint.** Uses angled screws through a drilled pocket hole — fast, strong, and beginner-friendly with an inexpensive jig.
5. **Miter joint.** Two pieces cut at matching angles (often 45 degrees) to form a corner without visible end grain — common in picture frames and trim.

## How to Choose the Right Joint

1. **For quick, functional projects** (shelves, boxes) — butt joints or pocket-hole joints are fast and strong enough for most uses.
2. **For furniture that needs a cleaner look** — rabbets and dadoes hide end grain and add strength without much added difficulty.
3. **For visible corners** (frames, trim) — miter joints give a clean, professional appearance.

## Common Mistakes

- Relying only on glue for butt joints without reinforcement (screws, nails, or pocket holes), leading to weaker joints over time.
- Cutting dadoes or rabbets slightly oversized, resulting in a loose, wobbly fit.
- Skipping a dry fit before gluing, making it harder to catch joint problems before they're permanent.

## Building Joinery Skills Progressively

Start with butt and pocket-hole joints, then work toward rabbets, dadoes, and miters as your comfort with measuring and cutting grows. A structured woodworking plans library typically sequences projects by joinery difficulty, making it easier to build these skills in a logical order.
