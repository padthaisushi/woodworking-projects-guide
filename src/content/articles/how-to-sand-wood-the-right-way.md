Sanding is often rushed, but it has a bigger visible impact on the final result than almost any other step — especially once stain or a clear finish is applied.

## Grit Progression Basics

Sandpaper grit numbers indicate coarseness — lower numbers are coarser, higher numbers are finer. A typical beginner progression:

1. **80–100 grit** — for removing rough mill marks or significant imperfections (skip this step if your wood is already smooth).
2. **120 grit** — general shaping and smoothing.
3. **150–180 grit** — refining the surface further.
4. **220 grit** — final pass before staining or finishing, for a smooth, even surface.

## Step-by-Step Method

1. **Always sand with the grain**, not across it — sanding against the grain leaves visible scratches that show up clearly once finish is applied.
2. **Work through grits in order.** Skipping grits (like jumping from 80 straight to 220) leaves deeper scratches that the finer grit can't fully remove.
3. **Wipe away dust between grits.** A tack cloth or slightly damp rag removes dust that can clog the next grit or get trapped under finish.
4. **Check under raking light.** Angle a light source across the surface to spot missed scratches or uneven areas before finishing.
5. **Ease sharp edges lightly.** A few light passes on edges and corners prevent finish from wearing through quickly at high-contact points.

## Common Mistakes

- Sanding across the grain, especially with power sanders, leaving visible swirl marks.
- Stopping at a coarse grit to save time, resulting in a rough or blotchy finished surface.
- Over-sanding softer woods like pine, which can create uneven density and blotchy stain absorption.

## Why This Step Matters So Much

Two identical projects can look completely different based on sanding quality alone — it's often the difference between an amateur look and a polished one. A structured woodworking plans library typically specifies the exact grit progression for each project, which takes the guesswork out of this step.
