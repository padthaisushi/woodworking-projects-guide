A board that cracks the moment the screw head pulls tight is one of the most common beginner frustrations. The good news is that splitting is predictable, and almost always preventable with a correctly sized pilot hole and a little attention to where the screw goes.

This guide explains why wood splits, gives a pilot hole size chart for the most common screw sizes, and covers what to do when a board has already cracked.

## Why Wood Splits When You Drive a Screw

A screw is a wedge. As the threads push into the wood, they force the fibers apart. In the middle of a board there is plenty of wood around the screw to absorb that pressure. Near an end or edge there isn't, so the fibers separate along the grain instead — and you get a crack.

Splits are more likely when:

- **The screw is close to the end of the board.** End grain has the least wood holding it together.
- **The wood is hard or dry.** Oak, maple, and kiln-dried trim boards resist compression, so they crack instead of squeezing.
- **The board is thin.** Fence pickets, 1/2" stock, and plywood edges have very little material around the screw.
- **The screw is large for the board.** A #10 screw in a 3/4" board near the end is asking for trouble.
- **Several screws line up along the same grain line.** Each one adds a little wedge, and together they open a crack.

## The Fix: Drill a Pilot Hole

A pilot hole removes some of the wood the screw would otherwise have to push aside. The threads still bite into the wall of the hole, so the screw holds — it just doesn't wedge the board apart.

For a joint where one board is screwed to another, there are actually two holes:

- **Pilot hole** — in the bottom board, about the size of the screw's solid core (the shaft without the threads).
- **Clearance hole** — in the top board, just larger than the threads, so the screw slides through and pulls the two boards tightly together instead of jacking them apart.

## Pilot Hole Size Chart

These are common starting points for standard wood screws. Screw brands vary, and so does wood, so treat this as a starting point and **always test on a scrap of the same board** before drilling your project.

| Screw size | Pilot hole (softwood) | Pilot hole (hardwood) | Clearance hole (top board) |
|---|---|---|---|
| #6 | 5/64" | 3/32" | 9/64" |
| #8 | 3/32" | 1/8" | 11/64" |
| #10 | 7/64" | 1/8" | 13/64" |

**How to check without a chart:** hold the drill bit in front of the screw. You should see the bit cover the solid core but leave the threads showing on both sides. If the bit hides the threads, it is too big and the screw will strip; if you can see the core around the bit, it is too small.

**Depth:** drill the pilot hole about as deep as the screw will go. A piece of masking tape wrapped around the bit makes a simple depth stop.

## Seven Habits That Prevent Splits

1. **Pre-drill near ends and edges, every time.** This alone prevents most splits.
2. **Move the screw back from the end.** If the design allows, an extra half inch of wood beyond the screw makes a big difference.
3. **Stagger screws.** Instead of putting several screws in a straight line along the grain, offset them slightly so they don't share the same fibers.
4. **Use the right size screw.** In thin stock, step down to a #6 or #8 and a shorter length rather than forcing a #10.
5. **Countersink in hardwood.** A countersink bit lets the screw head sit flush without pressing outward on the surface fibers as it seats.
6. **Lubricate in dense wood.** Rubbing the threads on a bar of wax or dry soap reduces the force needed to drive the screw.
7. **Don't overdrive.** Set the clutch on your drill/driver low and finish the last turn slowly. Driving too hard is a common cause of last-second cracks.

## What About "No-Split" Screws?

Many construction and deck screws have a notch or cutting tip that removes a little wood as they go in. They really do split softwood less often than plain-tip screws, and they are handy for quick outdoor projects. But they don't remove as much wood as a proper pilot hole, so for anything close to an end, in hardwood, or in a piece you care about, pre-drill anyway.

## How to Fix a Board That Already Split

1. **Stop and back the screw out.** Continuing to drive it will make the crack longer.
2. **Open the crack gently** — a putty knife or thin screwdriver is enough — and work wood glue deep into it. A straw or a piece of paper helps push glue into narrow cracks. Our [wood glue guide](../choose-the-right-wood-glue/) covers which glue to use indoors and outdoors.
3. **Clamp it closed** across the crack and wipe off the squeeze-out. Let it dry for the time on the glue label.
4. **Re-drill a correct pilot hole**, preferably in a slightly different position so the screw doesn't follow the same crack.
5. **If the split runs the full length** or goes through a load-bearing part, replace the board instead. A glued crack is fine for a box or a planter; it isn't something to trust on a part that holds weight.

## Safety Notes

- Wear safety glasses when drilling and driving screws. Bits can snap, and screws can kick out of a split board.
- Clamp small or thin pieces instead of holding them by hand. A drill bit that catches can spin the workpiece.
- Keep fingers out of the path of the bit — especially when drilling near the edge of a board, where the bit can break through the side.
