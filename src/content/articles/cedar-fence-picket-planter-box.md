Cedar fence pickets are one of the cheapest ways to buy rot-resistant lumber, and they are already thin and light enough to cut with basic tools. This plan turns three pickets and one 2x2 into a rectangular planter with only straight crosscuts — no table saw, no miters, no pocket holes.

The dimensions below are worked out from the most common picket size sold in the US: 5/8 inch thick, 5-1/2 inches wide, and 6 feet long. Pickets vary by store, so **measure yours before you cut** and adjust if the width or thickness is different.

## Finished Size

- About 24 inches long, 11-1/4 inches wide, and 11 inches tall
- Holds roughly one cubic foot of potting mix — enough for herbs, lettuce, or annual flowers

## What You'll Need

**Lumber**

- 3 cedar fence pickets, 5/8" x 5-1/2" x 6 ft (pick the straightest, driest ones in the stack)
- 1 cedar or pine 2x2, 8 ft long (actual size about 1-1/2" x 1-1/2")

**Hardware and supplies**

- 1-5/8" exterior-rated screws (about 60)
- Sandpaper, 80 or 120 grit
- Optional: landscape fabric and a staple gun, exterior wood glue, outdoor finish

**Tools**

- Saw (hand saw, jigsaw, circular saw, or miter saw)
- Drill/driver with a small pilot bit (about 1/8")
- Tape measure, pencil, and a square
- Two clamps (helpful, not required)
- Safety glasses and a dust mask

## Cut List

| Part | Qty | Length | Cut from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long side boards | 4 | 24" | Pickets 1 and 2 (two each) |
| End boards | 4 | 10" | Pickets 1 and 2 (two each) |
| Bottom slats | 4 | 9-7/8" | Picket 3 |
| Bottom cleats | 2 | 22-3/4" | 2x2 |
| Corner posts | 4 | 8-3/4" | 2x2 |

Each of the first two pickets gives you two 24" boards and two 10" boards (68 inches total), which leaves room to trim off the dog-eared top first. The 2x2 parts add up to about 81 inches, so one 8-foot piece is enough.

## Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Trim and cut the pickets.** Cut the dog-eared end off each picket so you start from a square end. Mark each part with a square, then cut the side boards, end boards, and bottom slats. Label them in pencil as you go.
2. **Cut the 2x2.** Cut two cleats at 22-3/4" and four corner posts at 8-3/4".
3. **Sand the cut ends.** Fence pickets are rough-sawn, and that texture is fine for a planter. Just knock the splinters off the cut ends and any sharp corners.
4. **Build the two long sides.** Lay two 24" boards edge to edge, rough face down. Place a cleat along the bottom edge, centered so there is a 5/8" gap at each end — that gap is where the end boards will sit. Pre-drill and drive three screws through the cleat into the lower board. Repeat for the other side.
5. **Add the corner posts.** Stand a post on top of each end of the cleat, with a 5/8" picket scrap between them as a spacer for the bottom slats. Keep the post in line with the end of the cleat, and screw it into both the lower and upper board. Pull the spacer out. The posts are what tie the two boards together, so use two screws per board.
6. **Attach the end boards.** Stand the two long sides up, cleats facing in. Fit the 10" end boards between them, against the ends of the cleats and posts. Pre-drill and screw through each end board into the posts (upper board) and into the end of the cleat and post (lower board). Check the box is square before driving the last screws.
7. **Drop in the bottom slats.** Slide the four slats in so they rest on the cleats, under the posts at each end. Space them evenly — you should have small gaps of about 1/4" for drainage. Screw each slat down into the cleats.
8. **Line and finish (optional).** Staple landscape fabric inside if you want to keep soil from washing through the gaps. Cedar can be left bare to weather to gray, or sealed on the outside with an exterior finish — see our [wood finishing guide](../how-to-finish-wood/) for the trade-offs.

## Safety Notes

- Wear safety glasses for every cut and a dust mask when cutting or sanding. Cedar dust irritates the nose and lungs for many people.
- Clamp the picket to a bench or sawhorse before cutting. Thin boards chatter and pinch the blade when they are held by hand.
- Always pre-drill. Pickets are thin and split easily when a screw goes in close to an end.
- If you are new to power saws, read our [woodworking safety tips](../woodworking-safety-tips-for-beginners/) before you start.

## Common Mistakes

- **Cutting everything before measuring the pickets.** If your pickets are 1/2" or 3/4" thick instead of 5/8", the cleat length and slat length change. The cleats should equal the long side length minus two picket thicknesses.
- **Buying wet pickets and building the same day.** Freshly stocked pickets are often damp and shrink as they dry, opening gaps between boards. Letting them dry for a few days, stacked flat with spacers, reduces this.
- **Driving screws close to the ends without a pilot hole.** This is the most common way to split a picket.
- **Setting the box directly on soil or a deck with no air underneath.** The bottom stays wet and rots first. Small feet made from 2x2 offcuts, or a couple of bricks, let it drain and dry.
- **Skipping the label check when growing food.** Not every fence picket is plain cedar — some are treated or pre-stained.

## Simple Variations

- **Longer box:** Change the long side boards to 36" and the cleats to 34-3/4". Add two more bottom slats and a fourth picket.
- **Taller box:** Add a third row of boards and lengthen the corner posts by the width of one picket. The extra soil is heavy, so add a slat or a center support under the bottom.
- **Cleaner look:** Screw from the inside, through the posts and cleats into the pickets, using 1-1/4" screws so the tips do not come through the face.
